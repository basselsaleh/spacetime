Spacetime · black hole lensing
About this view

A black hole in front of a made-up sky of stars, a galactic band and a few galaxies. It can spin (the Kerr solution), about the vertical axis. Every pixel's light ray is traced backwards from the camera through the curved spacetime, exactly: if it falls into the hole the pixel is black (the shadow); otherwise it shows the part of the sky the ray reaches.

Everything you see follows from that: the shadow, about 2.6 times the horizon's size; the Einstein ring when a galaxy lies right behind the hole; second, fainter images of the whole sky squeezed into a thin ring at the shadow's edge; and the distortion of the sky as you move.

Spin it up (a/M, up to 0.998, about the most an astrophysical black hole can reach) and the shadow shifts sideways and flattens on the side turning toward you, where light orbiting with the spin can pass closer to the hole; frame dragging also twists the sky around the axis. Seen from above the pole, the shadow stays round.

Drag to orbit, scroll or use the slider to change the distance (in units of the hole's mass M; without spin the horizon is at 2 M), double-click to reset. Close in, the shadow covers most of the sky; widen the field of view to see past it. The camera hovers at a fixed distance, turning with the spacetime itself (it has no angular momentum; it isn't orbiting or falling), and can't come closer than 3.5 M.
