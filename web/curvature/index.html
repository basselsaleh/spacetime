About this view

Space around a black hole, at one moment, cut through the hole's equator. That slice of space is curved, and this sheet is the shape a surface in ordinary 3D space needs so that every distance measured along it matches the real distance in the slice (Flamm's paraboloid, 1916).

Near the hole there is more room between two circles than their circumferences suggest: the grid squares stretch toward the center, and the sheet turns vertical at the horizon (r = 2 M), where it ends. Far away it keeps going, ever flatter, fading out of view.

The same sheet doesn't have to end at the horizon. The full solution continues it through a throat into a second, identical sheet: another outside region, another universe (Einstein and Rosen, 1935). The two join smoothly, with the throat as the narrowest circle. It can't be crossed: the throat pinches off faster than light could get through. Real black holes, formed from collapsing stars, have no second sheet.

Space inside and around a star of uniform density, at one moment, cut through its center. The glowing ball marks where the star is; the sheet under it is the star's own interior space, a smooth bowl (a piece of a sphere) with no hole and no edge. Outside the star the sheet is exactly the black hole's, the same well, because space outside depends only on the mass.

The curvature changes sign at the surface: inside, the bowl curves the same way in every direction (positive, like a sphere); outside, it is saddle-shaped (negative). Shrink the star and the bowl deepens; no star can be smaller than 9/4 M (Buchdahl's limit) without collapsing.

Only the sheet's own geometry is physical: lengths along it and its curvature, colored here. Its height and the direction "down" mean nothing; it is not a rubber sheet that things roll into, and it shows space, not time. (Falling comes mostly from time running slower near the mass, which no picture of space can show.)
